## 2.4 Linear Dependence and Span

A square matrix with linearly dependent columns is known as **singular**.

## 2.5 Norms

The $L^{p}$ norm is given by

$$
\lVert \mathbfit{x} \rVert_{p} = \left( \sum_{i}\lvert x_{i} \rvert ^{p} \right)^{1/p}
$$

for $p \in R,\ p\geq1$.

The term **norm** is not limited to $L^{p}$ norms, but rather describes any function $f$ mapping vectors to non-negative real numbers ($\mathbb{R}\;\backslash\;\mathbb{R}^{-}$) such that

- $f(\mathbfit{x})=0\implies \mathbfit{x}=\mathbf{0}$.
- $f(\mathbfit{x+y})\leq f(\mathbfit{x})+f(\mathbfit{y})$. (**triangle inequality**)
- $\forall\alpha \in \mathbb{R},\ f(\alpha \mathbfit{x})=\lvert \alpha  \rvert f(\mathbfit{x})$.

The $L^{2}$ norm is the **Euclidean norm**. The $L^{\infty}$ norm is the **max norm**, and is equivalent to $\lVert \mathbfit{x} \rVert_{\infty}=\mathrm{max}_{i}\lvert x_{i} \rvert$. Finally, the **Frobenius norm** is a non-$L^{p}$ norm described by

$$
\lVert A \rVert _{F}=\sqrt{ \sum_{i,j}{A_{i,j}}^{2} }
$$

which measures the size of a matrix $A$. It is analogous to the $L^{2}$ norm or matrices instead of vectors.

## 2.6 Special Kinds of Matrices and Vectors

An **orthogonal** matrix is a square matrix whose rows are mutually orthonormal and whose columns are mutually orthonormal.

$$
A^{\top}A=AA^{\top}=I
$$

This follows from the definition of orthonormality of vectors $\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{y}$.

- $\mathbfit{x}^{\top}\mathbfit{y}=0$.
- $\lVert \mathbfit{x} \rVert_{2}=1$.

Notably, matrix orthogonality implies that $A^{-1}=A^{\top}$.

## 2.7 Eigendecomposition

An **eigenvector** of a square matrix $\mathbfit{A}$ is a non-zero vector $\mathbfit{v}$ such that multiplication by $\mathbfit{A}$ alters only the _scale_ of $\mathbfit{v}$, and not the angle.

$$
\mathbfit{Av}=\lambda \mathbfit{v}
$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue** corresponding to this eigenvector $\mathbfit{v}$. By convention, we typically concern ourselves with only unit eigenvectors (since $\alpha\mathbfit{v}$ is still an eigenvector for any $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\;\backslash\;\{ 0 \}$).

Suppose a matrix $\mathbfit{A}$ has $n$ _linearly independent eigenvectors_ $\{ \mathbfit{v}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbfit{v}^{(n)} \}$ with eigenvalues $\{ \lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n} \}$. Let $\mathbfit{V}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbfit{v}^{(1)} & \dots & \mathbfit{v}^{(n)}\end{bmatrix}$ and $\boldsymbol\lambda=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1} & \dots & \lambda_{n}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}$. Then, the **eigendecomposition** of $\mathbfit{A}$ is

$$
\mathbfit{A}=\mathbfit{V}\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)\mathbfit{V}^{-1}
$$

Where $\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)$ is the diagonal matrix constructed by placing the elements of $\boldsymbol\lambda$ on the diagonals. Frequently, $\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)$ is represented by $\Lambda$.

Notably, every real symmetric matrix can be eigendecomposed with only real-valued eigenvectors and eigenvalues.

$$
\mathbfit{A}=\mathbfit{Q}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbfit{Q}^{\top}
$$

Where $\mathbfit{Q}$ is an orthogonal matrix composed of eigenvectors of $\mathbfit{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$ is a diagonal matrix. Since $\mathbfit{Q}$ is orthogonal, $\mathbfit{A}$ can be interpreted as scaling space by $\lambda_{i}$ in direction $v^{(i)}$.

Also, note that eigendecompositions are not necessarily unique; if two or more eigenvectors share the same eigenvalue, any set of orthogonal vectors lying in their span are also eigenvectors. Additionally, a matrix is also singular $\iff$ at least one eigenvalue is zero.

The eigendecomposition of a real symmetric matrix can also optimize quadratic expressions of the form $f(\mathbfit{x})=\mathbfit{x}^{\top}\mathbfit{A}\mathbfit{x}$ subject to the constraint $\lVert \mathbfit{x} \rVert_{2}=1$. The maximum value of $f$ is the maximum eigenvalue, and the minimum is the minimum eigenvalue.

Finally, we can characterize some matrices based on their eigenvalues.

- **Positive Definite:** $\lambda_{i}>0,\ \forall i$.
- **Positive Semidefinite:** $\lambda_{i}\geq0,\ \forall i$.
- **Negative Definite:** $\lambda_{i}<0,\ \forall i$.
- **Negative Semidefinite:** $\lambda_{i}\leq0,\ \forall i$.

Positive semidefinite matrices guarantee that $\forall \mathbfit{x},\ \mathbfit{x}^{\top}\mathbfit{Ax}\geq0$. Positive definite matrices additionally guarantee $\mathbfit{x}^{\top}\mathbfit{Ax}=0\implies \mathbfit{x}=\mathbf{0}$.

## 2.8 Singular Value decomposition

**Singular value decomposition (SVD)** is another method of factorizing a matrix, this time into **singular vectors** and **singular values**. SVD is more general than eigendecomposition because it applies to _all_ matrices, while eigendecomposition applies to only some square matrices. SVD has the following form

$$
\mathbfit{A}=\mathbfit{UDV}^{\top}
$$

Let $\mathbfit{A}$ be an $m\times n$ matrix. Then $\mathbfit{U}$ is an $m\times m$ matrix, $\mathbfit{D}$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, and $\mathbfit{V}$ is an $n\times n$ matrix. Additionally, $\mathbfit{U}$ and $\mathbfit{V}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\mathbfit{D}$ is a diagonal matrix.

The elements along the diagonal of $\mathbfit{D}$ are the **singular values** of the matrix $\mathbfit{A}$. The columns of $\mathbfit{U}$ are the **left-singular vectors**, and the columns of $\mathbfit{V}$ are known as the **right-singular vectors**.

We can further interpret/compute the SVD of $\mathbfit{A}$ via the eigendecomposition of related matrices. The left-singular vectors of $\mathbfit{A}$ are the eigenvectors of $\mathbfit{AA}^{\top}$, and the right-singular vectors are the eigenvectors of $\mathbfit{A^{\top}A}$. The non-zero singular values of $\mathbfit{A}$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $\mathbfit{A^{\top}A}$, which are also equivalent to the eigenvalues of $\mathbfit{AA^{\top}}$.

## 2.9 The Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse

Matrix inversion is undefined for non-square matrices. The **Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse** is designed to compute an inverse $\mathbfit{A}^{+}$ for a matrix $\mathbfit{A}$ in the equation $\mathbfit{Ax}=\mathbfit{y}$ that:

1. For wide matrices, produces a solution $\mathbfit{x}=\mathbfit{A^{+}y}$ with minimal Euclidean norm $\lVert x \rVert_{2}$ among all possible solutions. (Since wide matrices can have more than one solution)
2. For tall matrices, produces an $\mathbfit{x}=\mathbfit{A^{+}y}$ such that it minimizes the Euclidean norm $\lVert \mathbfit{Ax}-\mathbfit{y} \rVert_{2}$. (Since tall matrices can have zero solutions).

The pseudoinverse is defined formally as

$$
\mathbfit{A}^{+}=\lim_{ \alpha \to 0^{+} } (\mathbfit{A^{\top}A}+\alpha \mathbfit{I})^{-1}\mathbfit{A^{\top}}
$$

and practically calculated using the matrices in SVD

$$
\mathbfit{A}^{+}=\mathbfit{VD}^{+}\mathbfit{U}^{\top}
$$

Note that the psuedoinverse of any diagonal matrix, including $\mathbfit{D}$, can be computed by taking the reciprocal of its nonzero elements and then transposing the matrix.

> [!question] Why transpose?
> The matrix may not be square!

## 2.10 The Trace Operator

The trace operator represents the summation of a matrix's diagonal.

$$
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbfit{A})=\sum_{i}\mathbfit{A}_{i,i}
$$

It is a useful and interesting operator for several reasons. For instance, the Frobenius norm of a matrix can be expressed as

$$
\lVert \mathbfit{A} \rVert _{F}=\sqrt{ \mathrm{Tr}(\mathbfit{AA}^{\top}) }
$$

It is invariant under the transpose operator.

$$
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbfit{A})=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbfit{A}^{\top})
$$

And under "cyclic permutation" of a product.

$$
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbfit{ABC})=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbfit{CAB})=\mathrm{Tr}\mathbfit{BCA}
$$

Also, note that a scalar is its own trace.

$$
a=\mathrm{Tr}(a)
$$

## 2.11 The Determinant

The **determinant** $\det(\mathbfit{A})$ is a function mapping a square matrix $\mathbfit{A}$ to a real scalar. It is equivalent to the product of all the eigenvalues of the matrix, and can be interpreted as a measure of how much multiplication by the matrix $\mathbfit{A}$ dilates space. If $\det(\mathbfit{A})=0$, space is contracted completely along at least one dimension. If $\det(\mathbfit{A})=1$, the transformation preserves volume.
